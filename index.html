The question we asked

Singapore has built a strong missionary-sending culture. The infrastructure to welcome them home is largely invisible.

In 2025, Antioch21 and Asian Cross-cultural Training Institute (ACTI) asked 35 returned missionaries a simple but rarely-asked question: what was it actually like to come home? This brief summarises what they told us.

The people in this survey served for an average of 13 years — some for over 40. They returned to a Singapore that had moved on without them, often quietly and without ceremony, into churches and communities that had little framework for receiving them well.

A note on this data

This is a small, self-selected sample (n=35) drawn largely from a re-entry retreat. Percentages are directional signals, not statistical certainties. The full report names these limitations clearly. With those caveats noted, the findings are internally consistent and point strongly enough in certain directions to warrant serious attention.

Key findings

What the data tells us

01
The faith community gap is the most common wound
63% struggled to reconnect with their faith community — not theologically, but relationally. Missionaries come back changed. Most churches are not ready to receive that.
02
Emotional needs go unmet — often unrecognised
40% reported emotional or mental health struggles. Many only recognised the need months or years after return, triggered by secondary life events. Reactive support is not enough.
03
Financial pressure compounds everything else
37% cited financial strain. Singapore's housing costs and career gaps hit hard — particularly for those returning after a decade or more. The sending relationship tends to end the moment the missionary lands.
04
Families carry a compounded burden
For families, children's emotional adjustment, spousal wellbeing, and housing for larger households add layers that singles don't face. Support rarely extends meaningfully to spouses or children — even when they need it most.
05
Re-entry hits every area of life at once
It is not a housing problem, or a jobs problem, or a mental health problem. For most returnees, it is all of these simultaneously — and the weight of each compounds the others.

What made the difference

What actually helped

Re-entry retreats
Structured space to process, among people who understood. The simple act of being named and welcomed mattered deeply.
Close relationships
Friends who initiated without waiting to be asked — who listened without requiring explanation.
Financial buffers
Even a small transition allowance provided breathing room. Those without it described the financial pressure as consuming.
Church community
Not just the existence of a church — but specific people who took initiative and made intentional effort.

The support gap

Emotional & mental health support

Not charity — a bridge. A short-season allowance to resettle without the compounding pressure of immediate employment urgency.

Help translating field skills into terms local employers recognise. Particularly acute for those returning after 10+ years.

Advice for those coming home

We asked every respondent: what is one piece of advice you would give someone returning from the field? Across 35 responses, four themes emerged consistently.

On time & grace

"Repatriation takes time and grief comes in waves. Give yourself plenty of grace."

On community & connection

What this points toward

Possible ways forward

These directions are grounded in what the survey found. They are starting points — not final answers. Developing them into real, funded, scalable solutions requires deeper research and broader conversation.

Pastoral care
Proactive soul care — not reactive
Structured debriefing, counselling, and spiritual direction — available proactively and sustained for up to 2–3 years after return, not just at the moment of landing.
Financial
A financial bridge at the point of return
A short-season transition allowance gives returnees breathing room to resettle — treated as a natural extension of the sending relationship, not a discretionary add-on.
Community
Build the peer network — not just a retreat
34% named peer connection as an unmet need. 54% are already mentoring others. The supply and demand exist — what's missing is ongoing infrastructure to connect them.
Vocational
Skills translation for returning workers
Help returnees articulate and apply their field skills in a local context — especially critical for those returning after 10+ years.
Families
Extend care to the whole family
Spouses, children, and non-Singaporean partners face their own re-entry challenges. Family care must be explicit, not assumed.
What comes next

These directions point toward a larger question: what would it actually take to build these solutions well — at scale, with adequate funding, and with the sustained commitment needed to make a real difference? We are conducting a structured 4-month research project to find out.

A closing word

"God will not waste your pain. Returning back is a discipleship training. He is a good shepherd."

— Respondent, 11 years in East Asia

Every respondent who shared a hard story also shared evidence of resilience, faith, and ongoing fruitfulness. They are not a community in despair. They are a community waiting to be more fully seen, more intentionally supported, and more wisely deployed.

The same movement that built Singapore's strong missionary-sending culture is entirely capable of building the infrastructure to welcome its people home. With structured support in place, returned missionaries may not just survive re-entry, but become the most credible, experienced, and passionate advocates, mentors, and mobilisers for the next generation of goers.

"The same God who looked after you in the field will look after you when you return. Take lots of time and counsel to adjust."

Why this project

The 2025 survey named the problem clearly enough to act on. This project is about solving it.

The survey was a small, self-selected sample — powerful as a signal, limited as a foundation for policy or programme design. It confirmed that re-entry is hard and underserved. It could not tell us the full scale of the need, the nuanced texture of the gaps, or the realistic range of solutions that could address them.

Singapore's long-serving missionaries who built the foundations of the movement are returning. A younger generation is also returning after shorter deployments. Without deliberate investment in understanding and addressing their needs, the missions community risks losing both the wellbeing of returned workers and the mentoring capacity, institutional knowledge, and ongoing contribution they could offer.

Project purpose

This is a scoping project. Its primary output is not the solutions themselves, but the foundation — evidence, insight, and strategic options — on which lasting solutions can be built.

How we'll build knowledge

Three interconnected workstreams

Each workstream generates a distinct type of insight. Together, they produce a fuller picture of the problem and a credible set of forward paths.

A
Workstream A
Focus Groups with Returned Missionaries

3 to 4 focus groups of 6 to 8 participants each, structured around distinct profiles — moving beyond checkbox data into the lived, nuanced experience of return. We will reach beyond those who attended the 2025 retreat, specifically seeking out those with harder, more isolated re-entry stories.

Singles returning alone Families with children Long-term returnees (10+ years) Recent returnees (within 2 years)
Example questions we'll explore
"What were the moments of greatest struggle — and what triggered them?"
"What support did you actually receive, and how helpful was it in practice?"
"What did you wish existed that didn't?"
"What would you tell a church, agency, or funder who genuinely wanted to help?"
B
Workstream B
Practitioner Interviews with Church Leaders

8 to 12 in-depth semi-structured interviews (45–60 minutes each) with senior pastors, missions pastors, and missions committee chairs across a range of church sizes and denominational backgrounds. Priority is given to churches that have made intentional changes to how they receive missionaries home.

Example questions we'll explore
"What has your church learned about receiving missionaries home well — and what prompted you to act?"
"Where do you still feel you are falling short, despite your best efforts?"
"What would you do differently if you had more resources, time, or organisational support?"
"What could a coordinated network offer that individual churches cannot provide alone?"
C
Workstream C
Solutions Ideation & Strategic Scoping

A half-day ideation workshop (8–12 participants drawn from across the missions ecosystem) plus desk research benchmarking how comparable communities in the UK, USA, Australia, and South Korea have addressed returned missionary care. The explicit brief: think boldly — ask not just "what is realistic?" but "what would actually solve this?"

Example questions we'll raise
"What would a well-funded, professionally staffed re-entry support service actually look like?"
"Could a Singapore-based endowment fund for returned missionaries be viable — and what would it need to function?"
"Could technology — peer-matching platforms, online communities, digital resources — play a meaningful role?"
"What models from other countries are working, and what has proven unsustainable?"

A framework for solutions

Three tiers of possible response

Based on the existing survey data, potential solutions are likely to fall into three tiers. These are hypotheses to be tested and refined through the research — not predetermined conclusions.

Tier 1  ·  Immediate & Practical
Direct support to individuals within 12–24 months of return
The most urgent and visible needs — financial, pastoral, relational. Addressed through targeted, near-term interventions that require relatively low coordination overhead.
Financial bridge grants  ·  Counselling subsidies  ·  Peer matching  ·  Vocational coaching
Tier 2  ·  Systemic & Equipping
Building the capacity of churches and agencies to receive well
Shifting the problem upstream — equipping the communities and institutions that surround returned missionaries, so that individuals are better caught before they fall through the cracks.
Church equipping courses  ·  Re-entry toolkits  ·  Member care training  ·  Standards & accreditation
Tier 3  ·  Strategic & Enduring
Structural solutions that outlast any single programme cycle
The longest-horizon, highest-impact possibilities — requiring significant capital, coordination, and sustained commitment, but potentially transformative for the whole Singapore missions community.
Endowment fund for returned missionaries  ·  National advocacy  ·  Research infrastructure  ·  Policy change with agencies
